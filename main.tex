\section{Background and motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation it must publish the proposal, take public comments,
and show it considered them. A rule that ignores the record can be struck down in court. A
significant rule draws hundreds of thousands of comments. Three kinds arrive: \textbf{individual}
comments in a person's own words; \textbf{organized campaigns}, where a group drafts a letter and
thousands of real members send it; and \textbf{fabricated} comments, filed under names that never
consented. The first two are legitimate. The third is fraud. The second and third look the same:
large volumes of near-identical text in a short window.

The 2017 FCC net neutrality proceeding shows the stakes. Of about 22 million comments, the New York
Attorney General found about 18 million were fake: 8.5 million paid for by a broadband industry
coalition, 7.7 million from one college student on the other side, and 1.6 million from a source
never identified [5]. That took four years and subpoenas, for one proceeding.

Existing tools do not answer the question. Regulations.gov offers keyword search, and agencies
fold form letters into one ``Mass Comment Campaign'' record with a copy count, but nothing says
whether the senders were real. Form-letter detection is
solved when the letter is known: DURIAN reaches F1 0.98 on edited copies [1]. It stops there. Kao
found a 1.3M-comment FCC cluster generated by synonym swapping in which no two comments are
identical [2], so exact-match recall on it is zero. Weiss submitted 1{,}001 generated comments to a
federal site, and screened human judges identified them at 49\%, chance level [3]. LLM summaries do not fill the gap: four models agreed on stance within three points
but produced 137 to 237 themes on the same 1{,}260 comments [4]. No existing work
separates coordinated-but-genuine from fabricated, or reports a number for paraphrased campaigns.
We care because comment records are how the public is heard, and the current state lets fraud
and genuine campaigns be dismissed together.

\section{Problem statement}

Given a federal rulemaking docket, recover its comment campaigns and report, for each one, its size,
arrival pattern, the rule provisions it contests, and the evidence bearing on whether real people
sent it. The system describes campaigns and never labels an individual comment as fake.

\section{Scope and preliminary design}

\textbf{Objectives.} Each stage ships only if it beats the simplest method that could replace it.
No labelled corpus of fake comments exists, and hand-labelling individuals as fake is the harm we
refuse to cause. We label only template similarity, synthetic injections, the agency's own campaign
groupings, and findings made by third parties.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.13\textwidth}L{0.21\textwidth}L{0.15\textwidth}L{0.25\textwidth}L{0.15\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Our method} & \textbf{Baseline} & \textbf{Labelled with} & \textbf{Metric} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Ingest} & Tesseract, then docTR and a VLM & Tesseract & born-digital PDFs rendered at
200 dpi; 20 hand-typed scans & CER, \$/page \\
\addlinespace[2pt]
\textbf{Group} & MinHash, then embeddings + HDBSCAN & exact hash; MinHash alone & 300 labelled
pairs; 201 agency campaigns; paraphrase injected at 5 levels & pairwise F1; recall per level \\
\addlinespace[2pt]
\textbf{Characterize} & size, burstiness, metadata, variation structure (slot swaps vs.\ added
text) & text-only ranking & NY AG campaigns; Weiss's 1{,}001; Kao's spun cluster [2] & recall@$k$ \\
\addlinespace[2pt]
\textbf{Locate} & retrieval over rule text & keyword match & 50 hand-mapped campaigns & top-1
accuracy \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Headline comparison.} The FCC ECFS aggregation already returns the top 10 text clusters
(largest 240{,}592) and leaves 9{,}310{,}652 filings outside them. Quorum has to account for that
residue. If adding timing and metadata to text does not move known fake campaigns up the ranking,
the premise is wrong, and we report that. On Regulations.gov, where timing is day-level and
identity absent, the text has to carry it: genuine senders add their own words to a template, while
spun fakes swap among a few fixed phrases. The first test is whether that separates Kao's cluster
from genuine campaigns.

\textbf{Components claimed.} \emph{Large and heterogeneous data}: 11k to 24M records, two schemas,
text, PDF and scans. \emph{Scalability}: nothing may assume the 24M-filing corpus fits in memory.
\todo{confirm whether to also claim expensive inference (OCR + embeddings)}

\textbf{Learning emphasis.} Locality-sensitive hashing, density clustering, OCR evaluation, and
retrieval with chunking and a vector database, all methods the team can explain line by line.

\textbf{Application mock.} Three screens: docket overview, campaign explorer, evidence panel.

\textbf{Limitations and risks.} (1) Ingestion is bounded by the rate limit, so the 64k stretch
docket may not finish. (2) The FCC labels cover one proceeding and a handful of attributed campaigns, too
few for a rate, so results there are case by case. (3) The worst error is presenting a legitimate campaign as
fraud. Scores attach to campaigns only, every score shows its evidence, and the interface states
that coordination is normal. (4) Variation structure catches template-spun text, not comments an
LLM rewrites freely; for those we rely on injection tests and FCC identity.

\textbf{Fun factor.} We get to answer, with numbers, a question that took a state attorney general
four years.
