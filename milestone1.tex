\section{Background and Motivation}

Before changing a regulation, a federal agency must take public comments and respond to them. Big
rules draw hundreds of thousands, mostly form letters. A letter sent by 40{,}000 members of an
advocacy group is legitimate. The same letter filed under 40{,}000 stolen names is fraud, and the two
look identical on arrival. About 18 million of the 22 million comments on the FCC's 2017 net
neutrality repeal were fake, which New York's Attorney General established four years later by
subpoena [4].

Agencies group form letters but do not trace them. On our development docket, EPA's 2019 ``waters
of the United States'' rule, staff filed 507{,}230 comments under 201 campaign records, and 92 list
the sponsor as unknown. The stakeholders are agency staff who must answer the record, journalists
and attorneys general who audit it, and advocacy groups whose real members get discounted when
fraud surfaces. We were drawn in by Kao's analysis of the FCC comments [2] and the NY AG report [4].

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and evidence of whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source:} Regulations.gov and FCC ECFS APIs (public, free key); Federal Register API
        for rule text; the NY AG report [4] and Weiss's 1{,}001 machine-written comments [3] as labels.
  \item \textbf{Description:} Comment text with dates and PDF attachments.\\
        Development docket \texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149}: 11{,}444 records, 201 of them campaigns
        covering 507{,}230 comments. Validation docket FCC 17-108: 23{,}982{,}072 filings.
  \item \textbf{Key attributes:} text, receipt date, campaign copy count and sponsor, and the signer
        name, city and ZIP printed in campaign PDFs. ECFS adds filer name and address.
  \item \textbf{Relevance:} EPA's campaign records are free grouping labels, signer details let us
        check whether senders look real, and the FCC docket is the only one where fraud was proven.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns:} each comment needs its own API call (11.4 hours per key), so
        we split ingestion across five keys. 106 campaigns attach only a sample letter, so their senders
        cannot be checked. Dates have no time of day. All 13 PDFs we sampled had a text layer, so OCR
        is a fallback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

One Regulations.gov docket end to end, with the FCC docket for validation only. No fraud classifier,
and no individual gets a score.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Ingest comments and campaign PDFs into one record per comment.
  \item Group them into campaigns with MinHash [1], then embeddings and HDBSCAN for what MinHash misses.
  \item Characterize each campaign: size, daily arrival, how copies vary, and sender checks such as a
        city that does not match its ZIP.
  \item Link each campaign to the rule provisions it argues about, using RAG over the rule text.
  \item Serve it all as a web app for analysts.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success:} pairwise F1 of 0.95 or better against EPA's groupings, beating exact-match and
MinHash baselines; the NY AG's fake FCC campaigns ranked above genuine ones; and an analyst seeing
every campaign with its evidence on one screen instead of searching by keyword.

\section{Minimum Components}

\textbf{Large, heterogeneous data:} half a million letters across two APIs, in text and PDF.
\textbf{Scalability:} the FCC docket's 24 million filings are processed as a stream.

\section{Learning Emphasis}

Locality-sensitive hashing, density clustering, RAG with a vector database, evaluation without labels.

\section{Fun Factor}

EPA lists no sponsor for 92 campaigns. The first we opened (66{,}777 comments) was a CREDO Action
petition. We want to know who sent the other 91.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

Calling a genuine campaign fraudulent is the worst error, so scores describe campaigns and always
show their evidence. The FCC labels cover few campaigns, so results there are case studies. Comments
rewritten by an LLM may slip past grouping; we test this with paraphrased letters.
