\section{Background and Motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it has to publish the proposal, accept public comments,
and respond to them. The comments on one proposed rule make up a \emph{docket}. Large rules draw
hundreds of thousands of comments, and most are \emph{form letters}, where an organization (the
\emph{sponsor}) writes one text and asks supporters to send it. All the copies together form a
\emph{campaign}. A letter sent by 40{,}000 members of an advocacy group is legitimate. The same letter
under 40{,}000 stolen names is fraud, and the two look identical on arrival. In the FCC's 2017
net neutrality repeal, about 18 million of 22 million comments were fake. New York's Attorney General
established this four years later, with subpoenas [4].

Existing tools stop short of this. Regulations.gov offers keyword search, and agencies group form
letters by hand. On our development docket, EPA's 2019 ``waters of the United
States'' rule, staff filed 507{,}230 comments under 201 campaign records, 92 of them with no known
sponsor. Near-duplicate detection groups copies well [1] but misses reworded letters, and the 1.3
million likely fake FCC comments Kao found were all reworded [2]. None of these says who is behind a campaign
or whether its signers are real. Without that, agencies discount genuine campaigns along with fake
ones, and fraud is found years late, if at all.

The stakeholders are agency staff who must answer the record, the journalists and attorneys general
who audit it, and advocacy groups, whose real members lose their voice when fraud comes to light. Kao's analysis [2] and the NY AG report [4] drew us to the
problem.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and the evidence on whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source.} Public APIs of Regulations.gov and the FCC's Electronic Comment Filing
        System (ECFS), the Federal Register API for rule text, and two sets of known fakes, the NY AG
        report [4] and Weiss's 1{,}001 machine-written comments [3].
  \item \textbf{Description.} Comment text with receipt dates and PDF attachments.\\
        Development docket \texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149} has 11{,}444 records, including 201 campaign
        records that stand for 507{,}230 comments. The validation docket, FCC 17-108, has 23{,}982{,}072 filings.
  \item \textbf{Key attributes.} Text, receipt date, each campaign's copy count and sponsor, and the
        name, city and ZIP of every signer listed in campaign PDFs. ECFS adds filer name and address.
  \item \textbf{Relevance.} EPA's campaign records show which letters belong together, so they serve
        as free training and test labels. Signer details let us check whether a
        campaign's senders look real. An official investigation says which FCC 17-108 campaigns were fake, so we validate there.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns.} Each comment needs its own API call, 11.4 hours per key for
        the development docket, so we split ingestion across five keys. 106 campaigns attach only one
        sample letter, so their signers cannot be checked. Dates have no time of day. All 13 PDFs we
        sampled contain machine-readable text, so OCR is only a fallback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

We cover one Regulations.gov docket end to end and use the FCC docket only for validation. The
central challenge is that almost nothing is labelled fake, and labelling individuals ourselves would
risk the harm we want to avoid. We rely instead on labels that already exist (EPA's grouping, the NY
AG findings, Weiss's comments) and on reworded letters we generate.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments and campaign PDFs into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group} letters into campaigns. MinHash [1], a fast estimate of word overlap between
        texts, catches near-exact copies. The rest become vectors (\emph{embeddings}) clustered with
        HDBSCAN, a density-based method that finds groups of any size and leaves outliers out.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model.} Stock embeddings miss reworded copies, so we
        fine-tune the open model \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}
        with a contrastive loss, pulling letters from the same EPA campaign together and pushing
        letters from different campaigns apart. The 95 campaigns not marked as samples (up to
        441{,}802 letters) supply the training pairs. We hold out 20\% of campaigns entirely for testing.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by its size, arrival by day, how its copies differ
        (personal text added or words swapped), the sponsor named in its attachments, and signer
        checks such as a ZIP that does not match the stated city. Campaigns are ranked by how far this
        profile sits from the docket's typical campaign, so an analyst knows what to read first.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule provisions it argues about. Retrieval-augmented
        generation (RAG) finds the passages of the rule text closest to the campaign's letter, and an
        LLM names the provision, quoting the passage.
\end{enumerate}

We reuse an open embedding model, HDBSCAN and a hosted LLM, and build everything else.

\textbf{Success.} Grouping is scored with \emph{pairwise F1}. Over all pairs of letters, precision is
the share of pairs we put together that EPA also put together, and recall is the share of EPA's pairs
we recover. We aim for F1 of 0.95 or better on the held-out campaigns, above exact matching, MinHash
alone and the untuned model, including on letters reworded at five strengths.
On FCC 17-108 we measure \emph{recall@k}, the share of the NY AG's fake campaigns among our top $k$.
For an analyst, success is every campaign and its evidence on one screen instead of keyword search.

\section{Minimum Components}

\textbf{Large, heterogeneous data.} Half a million letters from two APIs, in text, HTML and PDF.
\textbf{Scalability.} FCC 17-108 has 24 million filings, so every step streams its input. \textbf{Complex model.} The fine-tuned embedding model, trained on
Vertex AI and retrained when a new docket is added.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

The worst error is making a genuine campaign look fraudulent, which would harm the group and every
real person who signed. So the app shows evidence about campaigns and never scores a person. The FCC labels cover only a few campaigns, so results there are
case studies. Mistakes in EPA's grouping would be learned by the model, so we hand-check a sample.
LLM-rewritten letters may escape grouping, and the rewording test measures how often.
