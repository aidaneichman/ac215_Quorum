\section{Background and Motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it has to take public comments and respond to them.
The comments on a rule are collected in a \emph{docket}, and on big rules most of them are
\emph{form letters}. An advocacy group, the \emph{sponsor}, writes one letter and asks its supporters
to send it, and all the copies together make up a \emph{campaign}.

That is normal when real people send the letter. It is fraud when the names are stolen, and on
arrival the two look the same. New York's Attorney General found that about 18 million of the 22
million comments on the FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal were fake, four years after the fact [4].

Agencies post one representative letter per campaign with a count of copies, as the Administrative
Conference of the United States recommended in 2021 [5]. On our development docket, EPA's 2019
``waters of the United States'' rule, that meant 507{,}230 comments filed under 201 campaign records,
92 with no known sponsor.

Near-duplicate detection groups exact copies [1, 6] but not reworded ones, and the 1.3 million likely
fake FCC comments Kao found were all reworded from one template [2]. Nothing available tells an
analyst who is behind a campaign or whether its signers look real, so genuine campaigns get written
off alongside fake ones. Our main user is the agency analyst who has to summarize and answer the
comment record, and journalists, attorneys general and advocacy groups benefit too. Kao's analysis
and the NY AG report drew us to the problem.

\paragraph{Problem statement} Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each
one's size, timing, arguments and evidence of whether real people sent it, without labelling any
individual comment as fake.

\section{Scope and Objectives}

A campaign is the set of comments sharing a template and a sponsor. EPA grouped by template, so its
grouping is our label, and cases where the two disagree are reported rather than forced. The central
challenge is recognizing one campaign across personalized and reworded letters without judging any
individual commenter. We reuse an open embedding model, HDBSCAN, BM25 and a hosted LLM, and build
the rest. Appendix B shows the architecture.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments, attachments and signer lists into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group} near-exact copies into one representative each with MinHash [6], a fast
        estimate of word overlap. We embed the representatives together with the remaining letters and
        cluster them with HDBSCAN, so a reworded letter lands with its template. For FCC 17-108 we
        cluster a sample stratified by MinHash group size and assign the rest to the nearest one.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model}, starting from \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5} [3]. It
        reads 512 tokens, enough for the typical 290-token letter and the personal text at its end, and learns with
        a contrastive loss on real letters, personalized copies as hard positives. It is
        cross-validated by leaving out one of the eight sponsor groups at a time.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by size, personalized share, any sponsor named in its
        attachments, timing where comments have their own dates, and signer checks such as ZIPs outside
        the stated state, names on several campaigns, or claimed versus listed counts. Features are scored against the docket median
        and averaged, and the ranking says what to read first. It is not a fraud score.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule sections it argues about, trying BM25 first, then
        embedding retrieval, and an LLM only if it beats both.
  \item \textbf{Serve} a dashboard and a live endpoint that returns a pasted comment's campaign,
        or none below a similarity threshold set on individual comments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Success Criteria}

\begin{leads}
  \item \lead{For the analyst} Every campaign and its evidence on one screen.
  \item \lead{Grouping} \emph{B-cubed F1}, averaging precision and recall over letters, on the
        held-out sponsor's personalized letters within a whole-docket clustering, against exact match,
        MinHash at 0.5 and 0.8, and the untuned model.
  \item \lead{Robustness} Letters reworded by an unseen LLM at five strengths, from 20\% to 100\% of
        sentences. The fine-tuned model should beat MinHash at each.
  \item \lead{Transfer} The same comparison on the Clean Power Plan docket, with its five unseen
        sponsors reported separately, and on Kao's FCC cluster [2]. If fine-tuning does not help
        there, we ship the untuned model.
  \item \lead{Ranking} How many of the NY AG's campaigns land in our top 20 on FCC 17-108, listing
        every false positive.
  \item \lead{Evidence and links} A hand audit of the 92 campaigns with no known sponsor, and top-1
        accuracy on 50 hand-mapped campaigns, at least half about a specific section.
\end{leads}

\section{Minimum Components}

\begin{leads}
  \item \lead{Large, heterogeneous data} Half a million EPA comments and 24 million FCC filings
        from three APIs, in text, HTML, PDF, spreadsheets and scans.
  \item \lead{Scalability} Sampled clustering keeps FCC 17-108 tractable, and a stateless API runs
        on Cloud Run and later GKE.
  \item \lead{Complex model} Few, noisy labels from 13 hand-grouped campaigns, and transfer
        to unseen dockets.
\end{leads}

\paragraph{Learning emphasis} Fine-tuning, RAG, containers and Vertex AI from the course, and
MinHash, density clustering and label-scarce evaluation from outside it.

\paragraph{Application mock} Appendix A uses real records. We built screen 3 by hand, and the
pipeline will produce it for every campaign.

\paragraph{Fun factor} We get to answer a question that took a state attorney general four years and
subpoenas. The first campaign with no known sponsor that we opened, 66{,}777 comments, turned out to
be a CREDO Action petition whose cover letter claims 110 more signers than its signer file lists.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Making a genuine campaign look fraudulent would harm the group and every real signer, so the
        app ranks campaigns, never people, shows the evidence behind each rank, and labels each sponsor as
        recorded by EPA or found in an attachment.
  \item The model would learn EPA's grouping mistakes, so we hand-check 200 letter pairs and 100
        letters flagged as personalized.
  \item The NY AG findings cover few campaigns, so FCC results are case studies, and if the download
        runs late we use those campaigns plus a random sample.
\end{itemize}
