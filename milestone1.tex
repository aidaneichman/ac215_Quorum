\section{Background and Motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it has to take public comments and respond to them.
The comments on a rule are collected in a \emph{docket}, and on big rules most of them are
\emph{form letters}. An advocacy group, the \emph{sponsor}, writes one letter and asks its supporters
to send it, and all the copies together make up a \emph{campaign}.

That is normal when real people send the letter. It is fraud when the names are stolen, and on
arrival the two look the same. New York's Attorney General found that about 18 million of the 22
million comments on the FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal were fake, four years after the fact [4].

Agencies handle campaigns the way the Administrative Conference of the United States recommended in
2021: post one representative letter with a count of copies [5]. EPA did this on our development
docket, its 2019 ``waters of the United States'' rule, filing 507{,}230 comments under 201 campaign
records and listing the sponsor as unknown for 92 of them.

Near-duplicate detection groups exact copies [1, 6] but not reworded ones, and the 1.3 million likely
fake FCC comments Kao found were all reworded from one template [2]. Nothing available tells an
analyst who is behind a campaign or whether its signers look real. Our primary user is the agency
analyst who has to summarize and answer the comment record; journalists, attorneys general and
advocacy groups benefit too. Kao's analysis and the NY AG report drew us to the problem.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and the evidence on whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source.} Public APIs of Regulations.gov, the FCC's Electronic Comment Filing System
        (ECFS) and the Federal Register. The NY AG report [4] names the FCC campaigns that were fake.
  \item \textbf{Description.} A \emph{record} is one Regulations.gov posting or ECFS filing, a
        \emph{comment} is one person's submission, a \emph{letter} is a comment whose full text we
        have, and a \emph{signer} is a name, city and ZIP in a signer list. The development docket
        (\texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149}) has 11{,}444 records standing for 518{,}473 comments: 11{,}243
        individual comments plus 201 campaign records covering 507{,}230 more. FCC 17-108 has
        23{,}982{,}072 filings.

  \item \textbf{Key attributes.} Per letter, its text and, for individual comments and ECFS filings,
        a receipt date. Per campaign record, one receipt date, a sponsor and a copy count. Per signer,
        name, city and ZIP. We keep no street addresses.
  \item \textbf{Relevance.} EPA's grouping labels about 114{,}000 letters with their campaign. About
        1{,}700 are \emph{personalized}, with the sender's own words added, and at a MinHash threshold
        of 0.8 only 7\% of those still match their template. \emph{Reworded} letters, rewritten by an
        LLM, form a separate stress test.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns.} One API call per record means 11.4 hours per key for the
        development docket, so we use five keys. ECFS pages by date slice: about 24{,}000 calls, 5
        hours across five keys. Four campaign PDFs are scans and need OCR. Signer names are hashed
        with a secret key at ingestion and never displayed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

We define a campaign as comments sharing a template and a sponsor. EPA's grouping follows templates,
so it is our label; where one sponsor runs two templates or two sponsors share one, we report the case
rather than force it. The central challenge is recognizing the same campaign across personalized and
reworded letters without making judgments about individual commenters. We reuse an open embedding
model, HDBSCAN, BM25 and a hosted LLM, and build the rest (Appendix B).

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments, attachments and signer lists into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group.} MinHash [6], a fast estimate of word overlap, collapses near-exact copies to
        one representative each. Representatives and all remaining letters are embedded and clustered
        together with HDBSCAN, so a reworded letter joins its template's group. On FCC 17-108 we
        cluster a stratified sample and assign the rest to the nearest cluster.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model}, \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5} [3] (512 tokens; the
        median letter is about 290 and personal text usually comes last), with a contrastive loss on
        real, deduplicated letters, personalized copies as hard positives. The 13 full campaigns come
        from seven named sponsors plus unknowns, so we use leave-one-sponsor-out cross-validation.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by size, share of copies with personal text, sponsor
        named in an attachment and, where signer lists exist, ZIP codes outside the stated state,
        names on several campaigns, and claimed versus listed signer counts. Timing is used only where
        each comment has its own date. Features are scored in median absolute deviations from the
        docket median, averaged, and ranked within groups of campaigns with the same features. The
        rank suggests what to read first; it is not a fraud score.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule sections it argues about: BM25 first, then
        embedding retrieval, and an LLM only if it beats both.
  \item \textbf{Serve} a dashboard and a live endpoint: paste a comment, get its campaign or ``none''.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Grouping.} \emph{B-cubed F1}, which averages precision and recall over letters, on the
        held-out sponsor's personalized letters, against exact match, MinHash at 0.5 and 0.8, and the
        untuned model.
  \item \textbf{Robustness.} Letters reworded by an unseen LLM at five strengths (20--100\% of
        sentences); the fine-tuned model should beat MinHash at every strength.
  \item \textbf{Transfer.} The same comparison on a second EPA docket held out entirely, the 2017
        Clean Power Plan repeal (\texttt{EPA-HQ-OAR-2017-0355}, 230 campaign records), and on Kao's
        FCC cluster [2]. If fine-tuning does not help there, we ship the untuned model.
  \item \textbf{Ranking.} Recall of the NY AG's campaigns in our top 20 on FCC 17-108, with every
        false positive listed.
  \item \textbf{Evidence and links.} Hand audit of the 92 unknown-sponsor campaigns; top-1 accuracy on 50
        hand-mapped campaigns, at least half about a specific section.
\end{itemize}

For the analyst, success is every campaign and its evidence on one screen instead of keyword search.

\section{Minimum Components}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Large, heterogeneous data.} Half a million EPA comments and 24 million FCC filings
        from three APIs, in text, HTML, PDF, spreadsheets and scans.
  \item \textbf{Scalability.} Sample-then-assign clustering for FCC 17-108; a stateless API that scales
        with users on Cloud Run, then GKE.
  \item \textbf{Complex model.} Few, noisy labels (13 full-text campaigns grouped by hand at EPA) and a
        model that must transfer to unseen dockets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Learning Emphasis}

From the course, fine-tuning, retrieval and RAG, containers and Vertex AI. From outside it, MinHash,
density clustering and clustering evaluation without clean labels.

\section{Application Mock Design}

Appendix A, filled in with real records. We built screen 3 by hand from one campaign's PDFs; the
pipeline will do it for every campaign.

\section{Fun Factor}

We get to answer, docket by docket, a question that took a state attorney general four years and
subpoenas. The first unknown-sponsor campaign we opened (66{,}777 comments) turned out to be a CREDO
Action petition whose cover letter claims 110 more signers than its signer file lists.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Making a genuine campaign look fraudulent would harm the group and every real signer. The app
        ranks campaigns, never people, shows the evidence behind each rank, says whether a sponsor was
        recorded by EPA or found in an attachment, and corrects any display a named group disputes.
  \item The model would learn EPA's grouping mistakes, so we hand-check 200 letter pairs. With only
        13 full-text campaigns, the transfer test decides whether fine-tuning ships.
  \item The NY AG findings cover few campaigns, so FCC results are case studies. If the FCC download
        slips, we use the named campaigns plus a random sample of other filings.
\end{itemize}
