\section{Background and Motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it has to take public comments and respond to them.
The comments on a rule are collected in a \emph{docket}, and on big rules most of them are
\emph{form letters}. An advocacy group, the \emph{sponsor}, writes one letter and asks its supporters
to send it, and all the copies together make up a \emph{campaign}.

That is normal when real people send the letter. It is fraud when the names are stolen, and on
arrival the two look the same. New York's Attorney General found that about 18 million of the 22
million comments on the FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal were fake, four years after the fact [4].

The closest existing practice is what the Administrative Conference of the United States recommended
in 2021: post one representative letter per campaign with a count of copies [5]. EPA did exactly that
on our development docket, its 2019 ``waters of the United States'' rule, filing 507{,}230 comments
under 201 campaign records and listing the sponsor as unknown for 92 of them. Near-duplicate detection
automates grouping for exact copies [1] but not for reworded ones, and the 1.3 million likely fake FCC
comments Kao found were all reworded from one template [2]. Kao's embedding analysis was a one-off
study of one docket. None of these tell an analyst who is behind a campaign or whether its signers
look real, so genuine campaigns get written off alongside fake ones and fraud surfaces years later.

Our primary user is the agency analyst who has to summarize and answer the comment record.
Journalists and attorneys general who audit the record, and advocacy groups whose real members get
discounted, benefit as well. Kao's analysis [2] and the NY AG report [4] drew us to the problem.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and the evidence on whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source.} Public APIs of Regulations.gov, the FCC's Electronic Comment Filing System
        (ECFS) and the Federal Register. Known fakes from the NY AG report [4] and Weiss's public
        dataset of 1{,}001 bot comments with matching human comments [3].
  \item \textbf{Description.} We use four units. A \emph{record} is one Regulations.gov posting, a
        \emph{comment} is one person's submission, a \emph{letter} is a comment whose full text we
        have, and a \emph{signer} is a name, city and ZIP in a campaign's signer list. The development
        docket (\texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149}) has 11{,}444 records standing for 518{,}473 comments:
        11{,}243 individual comments and 201 campaign records covering 507{,}230 more. Of those
        campaigns, 13 attach every letter (about 116{,}000), 8 attach a signer list or spreadsheet
        (295{,}647 comments) and 180 attach one sample letter or nothing. ECFS lists 23{,}982{,}072 filings
        for FCC 17-108, more than the 22 million comments the NY AG counted, and Kao estimated fewer
        than 800{,}000 distinct texts among them [2].
  \item \textbf{Key attributes.} Letter text, receipt date, sponsor and copy count per campaign, and
        signer name, city and ZIP. ECFS adds filer name, ZIP and timestamp; we keep no street addresses.
  \item \textbf{Relevance.} EPA's grouping labels about 116{,}000 letters with their campaign, and the
        copies are not all identical: in six of the full campaigns, about 2{,}000 letters differ from
        their template by more than 10\% in length because senders added their own words. Signer lists let
        us check whether a campaign's senders look real. The NY AG findings say which FCC campaigns
        were fake, so FCC 17-108 is our external test.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns.} Fetching one comment takes one API call, 11.4 hours per key for
        the development docket, so we use five keys and start the FCC bulk download in October.
        180 campaigns have no copies to analyze, so we use only their counts. Receipt dates have no
        time of day. Four campaign PDFs are scans, so OCR is needed only there. Signer lists are personal
        data, so names are hashed at ingestion and only campaign-level totals are ever shown.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

One Regulations.gov docket end to end, with FCC 17-108 used only for external validation. We define a
campaign as comments sharing a template and a sponsor. EPA's grouping follows templates, so it is our
label, and where one sponsor runs two templates or two sponsors share one, we report the case rather
than force it. The central challenge is recognizing the same campaign across personalized and reworded letters
without making judgments about individual commenters. We train only on EPA's grouping and keep the NY AG
findings, Weiss's comments and Kao's cluster untouched until final evaluation. We reuse an open
embedding model, HDBSCAN, BM25 and a hosted LLM, and build the rest (architecture in Appendix B).

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments, attachments and signer lists into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group.} MinHash [1], a fast estimate of word overlap, collapses near-exact copies to one
        representative each. Representatives and all remaining letters are then embedded and clustered
        together with HDBSCAN, so a reworded letter joins the group of its template. At FCC scale this
        runs on distinct texts, not on 24 million filings.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model.} We fine-tune \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} with a
        contrastive loss that pulls letters from the same EPA campaign together and pushes other
        campaigns apart. Training pairs are real letters only, with personalized copies as hard
        positives; no LLM rewordings are used in training. The 13 full campaigns come from seven
        named sponsors and two unknown ones, so we use leave-one-sponsor-out cross-validation and
        report the spread across folds.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by size, the share of comments arriving on its busiest day,
        the share of copies with personal text, whether a sponsor is named in its attachments, and
        signer checks: ZIP codes outside the stated state, names that also sign other campaigns, and
        claimed versus listed signer counts. Each feature is measured in median absolute deviations
        from the docket's median, and campaigns with the largest total rank first for review. This is
        an interpretable heuristic, not a fraud score; campaigns with no copies are ranked on size,
        timing and sponsor only.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule sections it argues about. BM25 keyword retrieval over
        Federal Register sections is the baseline, then embedding retrieval, and an LLM is added only
        if it beats both.
  \item \textbf{Serve} a dashboard and one live endpoint: paste a comment and get its closest campaign
        and similarity from the fine-tuned model.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success.} Grouping is scored with \emph{B-cubed F1}, which averages precision and recall over
letters and avoids the quadratic weight pairwise F1 gives large campaigns. The headline result is
on the naturally personalized letters, with all letters and a per-campaign breakdown alongside, each
against exact matching, MinHash and the untuned model. Reworded
test letters come from an LLM never used in training, at five strengths (20\% to 100\% of sentences
rewritten). The fine-tuned model must beat MinHash on reworded letters at every strength without
losing ground on exact copies. On FCC 17-108 we map each campaign the NY AG names to its ECFS template by text
search, then report precision and recall in our top 20, how many campaigns were ranked, and every
false positive. Sponsor evidence
is audited by hand on the 92 unknown-sponsor campaigns, and provision links by top-1 accuracy on 50
hand-mapped campaigns, at least half of which argue about a specific section. For the analyst, success is every campaign and its evidence on one screen
instead of keyword search.

\section{Minimum Components}

\textbf{Large, heterogeneous data.} Half a million EPA comments and 24 million FCC filings from three
APIs, in text, HTML, PDF, spreadsheets and scans. \textbf{Scalability.} Grouping works on distinct texts, so FCC 17-108 shrinks
from 24 million filings to under a million; the API is stateless and scales with users on Cloud Run,
then GKE. \textbf{Complex model.} The fine-tuned embedding model, trained on Vertex AI and retrained
when a new docket arrives with agency campaign records.

\section{Learning Emphasis}

From the course, fine-tuning, retrieval and RAG, containers and Vertex AI. From outside it,
MinHash, density clustering and clustering evaluation without clean labels.

\section{Fun Factor}

We get to answer, docket by docket, a question that took a state attorney general four years and
subpoenas. The first unknown-sponsor campaign we opened (66{,}777 comments) turned out to be a CREDO
Action petition whose cover letter claims 110 more signers than its signer file lists.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

Making a genuine campaign look fraudulent would harm the group and every real person who signed. The
app scores campaigns, never people, shows the evidence behind every rank, and labels a sponsor as
recorded by EPA or found in an attachment. The model would learn EPA's grouping mistakes, so we
hand-check 200 letter pairs and adjudicate disagreements. Only 13 EPA campaigns contain every letter, so there are many letters but few independent
campaigns; if fine-tuning does not beat the untuned model on held-out sponsors, we keep the simpler
model. The FCC labels cover few campaigns, so results there are case studies. Freely LLM-rewritten letters may still escape grouping, and the
rewording test measures how often. If the full FCC download is not done by MS4, we validate on the
campaigns the NY AG named plus a random sample of other filings.
