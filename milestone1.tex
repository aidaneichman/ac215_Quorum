\section{Background and Motivation}

\textbf{How comments work.} Before changing a regulation, a federal agency must take public comments
and respond to them. The comments on one rule form a \emph{docket}. Most are \emph{form letters}, one
text that an organization (the \emph{sponsor}) asks supporters to send. All copies together form a
\emph{campaign}.

\textbf{What goes wrong.} A letter sent by 40{,}000 real members is legitimate. The same letter under
40{,}000 stolen names is fraud, and the two look identical on arrival. About 18 million of the 22
million comments on the FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal were fake. New York's Attorney General
established this four years later [4].

\textbf{The gap.} Regulations.gov offers keyword search, and agencies group form letters by hand. On
our development docket, EPA's 2019 ``waters of the United States'' rule, staff filed 507{,}230
comments under 201 campaign records, 92 with no known sponsor. Near-duplicate detection groups copies
[1] but misses reworded ones, and the 1.3 million likely fake FCC comments Kao found were all reworded
[2]. Nothing says who is behind a campaign or whether its signers are real. Genuine campaigns get
discounted with fake ones, and fraud surfaces years late.

\textbf{Stakeholders.} Agency staff who must answer the record, the journalists and attorneys general
who audit it, and advocacy groups whose real members lose their voice. Kao's analysis [2] and the NY
AG report [4] drew us to the problem.

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and the evidence on whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source.} Public APIs of Regulations.gov, the FCC's Electronic Comment Filing System
        (ECFS) and the Federal Register (rule text). Known fakes from the NY AG report [4] and
        Weiss's 1{,}001 machine-written comments [3].
  \item \textbf{Description.} Comment text with receipt dates and PDF attachments. The development
        docket has 11{,}444 records, 201 of them campaigns standing for 507{,}230 comments
        (\texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149}). Validation docket FCC 17-108 has 23{,}982{,}072 filings.
  \item \textbf{Key attributes.} Text, receipt date, campaign copy count and sponsor, and each
        signer's name, city and ZIP from campaign PDFs. ECFS adds filer name and address.
  \item \textbf{Relevance.} EPA's campaign records are free training and test labels. Signer details
        show whether a campaign's senders look real. The NY AG named which FCC 17-108 campaigns were
        fake, so we validate there.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns.} One API call per comment takes 11.4 hours per key for the
        development docket, so we use five keys. 106 campaigns attach only a sample letter, so their
        signers cannot be checked. Dates have no time of day. All 13 PDFs we sampled have
        machine-readable text, so OCR is a fallback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

One Regulations.gov docket end to end, with FCC 17-108 for validation. The central challenge is that
almost nothing is labelled fake, and labelling people ourselves risks the harm we want to avoid. We
use existing labels (EPA's grouping, the NY AG findings, Weiss's comments) and reworded letters we
generate. We reuse an open embedding model, HDBSCAN and a hosted LLM, and build the rest.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments and campaign PDFs into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group} letters with MinHash [1], a fast estimate of word overlap that catches
        near-exact copies. The rest become vectors (\emph{embeddings}) clustered with HDBSCAN, which
        finds groups of any size and leaves outliers out.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model.} Stock embeddings miss reworded copies, so we
        fine-tune the open model \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} with a contrastive loss that pulls letters from the same
        EPA campaign together and pushes other campaigns apart, using the 95 campaigns not marked as
        samples (up to 441{,}802 letters) and holding out 20\% of campaigns for testing.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by size, arrival by day, how copies differ (added text
        or swapped words), the sponsor named in its attachments, and signer checks such as a ZIP that
        does not match the city. Campaigns furthest from the docket's typical profile rank first.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the provisions it argues about. RAG (retrieval-augmented
        generation) finds the closest passages of the rule, and an LLM names the provision.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success.} \emph{Pairwise F1} against EPA's grouping, where precision is the share of letter
pairs we group that EPA also grouped and recall is the share of EPA's pairs we find. Target 0.95 on
held-out campaigns, above exact matching, MinHash and the untuned model, including on letters reworded
at five strengths. On FCC 17-108, \emph{recall@k}, the share of the NY AG's fake campaigns in our
top $k$. For an analyst, every campaign and its evidence on one screen instead of keyword search.

\section{Minimum Components}

\textbf{Large, heterogeneous data.} Half a million letters from two APIs, in text, HTML and PDF.
\textbf{Scalability.} FCC 17-108 has 24 million filings, so every step streams its input.
\textbf{Complex model.} The fine-tuned embedding model, trained on Vertex AI and retrained per docket.

\section{Learning Emphasis}

From the course, fine-tuning, RAG with a vector database, containers and Vertex AI. From outside it,
MinHash, density clustering and evaluation with few labels.

\section{Fun Factor}

EPA lists no sponsor for 92 campaigns. The first one we opened (66{,}777 comments) was a CREDO Action
petition.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

Making a genuine campaign look fraudulent would harm the group and every real person who signed, so
the app shows evidence about campaigns and never scores a person. The FCC labels cover few campaigns,
so results there are case studies. The model would learn any mistakes in EPA's grouping, so we
hand-check a sample. LLM-rewritten letters may escape grouping; the rewording test measures how often.
