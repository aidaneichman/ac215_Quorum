\section{Background and Motivation}

Before changing a regulation, a federal agency must publish the proposal, take public comments, and
respond to them. All comments on one proposed rule form a \emph{docket}. Big rules draw hundreds of
thousands of comments, mostly \emph{form letters}: one text that an organization (the
\emph{sponsor}) asks many people to send, which together make a \emph{campaign}. A letter sent by
40{,}000 members of an advocacy group is legitimate. The same letter filed under 40{,}000 stolen
names is fraud, and the two look identical on arrival. About 18 million of the 22 million comments on
the FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal were fake, which New York's Attorney General established four
years later by subpoena [4].

Agencies group form letters but do not trace them. On our development docket, EPA's 2019 ``waters
of the United States'' rule, staff filed 507{,}230 comments under 201 campaign records, and 92 list
the sponsor as unknown. The stakeholders are agency staff who must answer the record, journalists
and attorneys general who audit it, and advocacy groups whose real members get discounted when
fraud surfaces. We were drawn in by Kao's analysis of the FCC comments [2] and the NY AG report [4].

\section{Problem Statement}

Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each one's size, timing, arguments,
and evidence of whether real people sent it, without labelling any individual comment as fake.

\section{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Source:} the public APIs of Regulations.gov (federal agency dockets) and the FCC's
        Electronic Comment Filing System (ECFS); the Federal Register API for rule text; the NY AG
        report [4] and Weiss's 1{,}001 machine-written comments [3] as known cases of fake comments.
  \item \textbf{Description:} comment text with receipt dates and PDF attachments.\\
        Development docket \texttt{EPA-HQ-OW-2018-0149}: 11{,}444 records, 201 of them campaign
        records standing for 507{,}230 comments. Validation docket FCC 17-108: 23{,}982{,}072 filings.
  \item \textbf{Key attributes:} text, receipt date, each campaign's copy count and sponsor, and the
        name, city and ZIP of every signer listed in campaign PDFs. ECFS adds filer name and address.
  \item \textbf{Relevance:} EPA's campaign records tell us which letters belong together, so they
        serve as training and test labels at no labelling cost. Signer details let us check whether a
        campaign's senders look real. FCC 17-108 is the only docket where fraud was proven, so we
        validate there.
  \item \textbf{Data quality concerns:} each comment needs its own API call, 11.4 hours per API key
        for the development docket, so we split ingestion across five keys. 106 campaigns attach only
        one sample letter, so their signers cannot be checked. Dates have no time of day. All 13 PDFs
        we sampled contain machine-readable text, so OCR (reading text from scanned images) is a
        fallback.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

One Regulations.gov docket end to end, with the FCC docket for validation only. We do not build a
fraud classifier, and no individual comment or person gets a score. The pipeline has five steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments and campaign PDFs into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group} letters into campaigns in two passes. MinHash [1], a fast way to estimate the
        word overlap between two texts, catches exact and near-exact copies. For what is left, we
        embed each letter as a vector (an \emph{embedding}, from a sentence-embedding model) and
        cluster the vectors with HDBSCAN, which finds groups of any size and leaves outliers
        unassigned.
  \item \textbf{Train our own embedding model.} Off-the-shelf embeddings are not trained to see a
        reworded form letter as the same campaign. We fine-tune an open sentence-embedding model
        (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) with a contrastive loss, which pulls letters from the same EPA
        campaign together and pushes letters from different campaigns apart. The 95 campaigns that
        attach their copies (up to 441{,}802 letters) supply these pairs without hand labelling. We hold out
        20\% of campaigns, split by campaign so no test letter's campaign was seen in training.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign with measured evidence: size, arrival by day, how copies
        differ (personal text added versus words swapped), sponsor named in the attachment, and
        signer checks such as a ZIP that does not match the stated city. Campaigns are ranked by how
        far this profile sits from the docket's typical campaign, to show an analyst what to read first.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule provisions it argues about, using retrieval-augmented
        generation (RAG): we retrieve the most relevant passages of the rule text and have an LLM
        name the provision, citing the passage.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success.} Grouping is scored with \emph{pairwise F1}: over every pair of letters, precision
is the share of pairs we put together that EPA also put together, and recall is the share of EPA's
pairs we recover. Targets: F1 of 0.95 or better on held-out campaigns, beating exact matching, MinHash
alone and the untuned model, including on letters automatically reworded at five strengths. On FCC 17-108, the NY AG's fake
campaigns should appear in our top-ranked campaigns (\emph{recall@k}, the share found in the top
$k$). For an analyst, success is every campaign and its evidence on one screen instead of
keyword search.

\section{Minimum Components}

\textbf{Large, heterogeneous data:} half a million letters across two APIs, in text, HTML and PDF.
\textbf{Scalability:} FCC 17-108 has 24 million filings, so every step streams its input and never
loads a whole docket into memory. \textbf{Complex model:} the fine-tuned embedding model, trained on
Vertex AI and retrained when a new docket is added.

\section{Learning Emphasis}

Near-duplicate detection with MinHash, density clustering, contrastive fine-tuning, RAG with a vector
database, and evaluation when most answers have no label.

\section{Fun Factor}

EPA lists no sponsor for 92 campaigns. The first one we opened (66{,}777 comments) was a CREDO Action
petition.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

Calling a genuine campaign fraudulent is the worst error, so rankings describe campaigns and always
show their evidence. The FCC labels cover few campaigns, so results there
are case studies. EPA's groupings may contain mistakes, which would train them into the model; we
hand-check a sample. Letters rewritten freely by an LLM may still escape grouping, and the rewording
test measures how often.
