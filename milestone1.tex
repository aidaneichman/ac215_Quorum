\section{Background and motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it publishes the proposal, takes public comments, and
has to respond to the substance of what it receives. A contested rule can draw hundreds of
thousands of comments, most of them form letters. Form letters are a normal part of this: an
advocacy group drafts one, its members sign it, and the agency hears from forty thousand real
people. The trouble is that a fabricated campaign, filed under the names of people who never wrote
anything, looks the same when it arrives.

This has happened at scale. In the FCC's 2017 net neutrality proceeding, the New York Attorney
General found that about 18 million of 22 million comments were fake: 8.5 million paid for by a
broadband industry group, 7.7 million from one college student on the other side, and 1.6 million
never traced [5]. Establishing that took four years and subpoenas, for a single docket. Kao had
spotted the largest fake cluster in 2017 from public data alone: 1.3 million comments made by
swapping synonyms into a template, so that no two were identical [2]. Weiss later submitted 1{,}001
machine-generated comments to a federal site, and screened human readers told them apart from real
ones 49\% of the time, no better than chance [3].

Agencies today group comments, but they do not trace where they came from. On our development
docket, EPA's 2019 proposal redefining ``waters of the United States'', staff folded 507{,}230
form-letter comments into 201 ``Mass Comment Campaign'' records, and 92 of those list the sponsor as
unknown. Grouping known form letters is close to solved, with F1 around 0.98 [1]. What is missing is
what comes after: who is behind a campaign, whether its senders look like real people, and what it
objects to. LLM summaries do not settle this. Four models run on the same
1{,}260 comments agreed on stance to within three points but produced anywhere from 137 to 237
themes [4].

The stakeholders are the agency staff who must read and answer the record, the journalists,
inspectors general and attorneys general who audit it, and the advocacy groups whose real members
get discounted every time a fake campaign comes to light. The idea came out of reading Kao's
analysis and the NY AG report, which each answered the question for one docket, by hand, years late.

\section{Problem statement}

For a federal rulemaking docket, find the comment campaigns in it and report, for each one, its size,
how it arrived, what it argues, and the evidence on whether real people sent it, without ever
labelling an individual comment as fake.

city and ZIP inside campaign PDFs; filer name, address and timestamp on ECFS.

\textbf{Relevance.} The agency's own campaign records give us grouping labels without hand
annotation. The names and ZIP codes inside them let us check, per campaign, whether senders look like
real people. The FCC docket is the one place where fraud was established independently, so it is
where we test whether our evidence points the right way.

\section{Scope and preliminary design}

\textbf{Scope and objectives.} One Regulations.gov docket end to end, with the FCC docket used only
for validation. We do not build a fraud classifier and never score a person. Each of the four steps
has to beat the simplest thing that could replace it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments and campaign PDFs into one record per comment.
  \item \textbf{Group} comments into campaigns: MinHash first, embeddings with HDBSCAN only for what
        MinHash leaves unmatched.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign: size, daily arrival, how copies vary (added personal
        text versus swapped phrases), and sender checks such as city and ZIP disagreeing or the same
        name signing several campaigns.
  \item \textbf{Locate} the rule provisions each campaign argues about, by retrieval over the rule text.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Success.} On the model side, reproduce EPA's grouping at pairwise F1 of 0.95 or better, find
campaigns among the 11{,}243 records EPA left ungrouped, and rank the known FCC fakes above genuine
campaigns. For comparison, the ECFS API already returns the ten largest text clusters and leaves
9{,}310{,}652 filings outside them. If adding timing and sender checks to text does not move known
fakes up the ranking, the premise is wrong, and we will report that. For the analyst, success means
opening a docket and seeing every campaign with its size, arrival, arguments and evidence on one
screen, where today the record offers keyword search and a list of campaign titles.

\textbf{Components.} \emph{Large and heterogeneous data}: half a million letters across two APIs
with different schemas, in text, HTML and PDF. \emph{Scalability}: the FCC docket has 24 million
filings, so every stage streams and nothing assumes a docket fits in memory.

\textbf{Learning emphasis.} Locality-sensitive hashing, density clustering, retrieval with chunking
and a vector database, and evaluation without ground truth. We use an off-the-shelf embedding model
and Tesseract. What we build is the ingestion, grouping, campaign features, retrieval and evaluation.

\textbf{Application mock.} Three screens: docket overview, campaign explorer, evidence for one
campaign.

\textbf{Fun factor.} Most of this data has never been looked at closely. EPA lists no sponsor for 92
of the campaigns on our docket, and we would like to know who sent them.

\textbf{Limitations, risks and ethics.} The worst mistake would be presenting a real campaign as fraud,
since that discredits people who took part in their own government. Scores therefore attach to
campaigns, every score shows its evidence, and the interface says plainly that coordination is
normal. The FCC labels cover one proceeding and a handful of campaigns, too few for a rate, so
results there are reported case by case. Swapped-phrase detection will miss comments an LLM rewrites
freely; the paraphrase tests measure how much.

\textbf{Milestones.} \emph{Late Sep:} this proposal; dev docket ingestion starts.
\emph{Mid Oct:} containerized one-command pipeline, data versioning, retrieval flow, grouping
baseline, 300 labelled pairs. \emph{Mid Nov:} experiment tracking, Vertex AI, Cloud Run, documented
API, grouping results with paraphrase tests. \emph{Early Dec:} FastAPI backend and frontend, CI and
tests, campaign features, FCC evaluation. \emph{Mid Dec:} Kubernetes scaling, automated retraining,
video and blog post.
